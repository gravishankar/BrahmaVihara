Mettā
Loving-friendliness
The foundation of all four abodes. An unconditional warmth for all beings — neither possessive attachment nor indifference, but the open wish: may you be well.
Near enemy
Possessive attachment — caring with conditions
Far enemy
Hatred or ill-will
Traditional phrases · Bhante G

May I be gentle. May I be relaxed. May I be happy and peaceful.
May I be healthy. May my heart become soft.
May my words be pleasing. May my actions be kind.

May all beings be well, happy, and peaceful.
May no harm come to them. May no difficulties come to them.
May they have patience, courage, understanding, and determination
to meet and overcome inevitable difficulties in life.

May all beings in all ten directions be well, happy, and peaceful.
May all beings everywhere be filled with loving-friendliness —
abundant, exalted, and measureless.

Working with the near enemy
Pure mettā has no agenda. The moment you feel hurt when someone chooses differently — you've slipped into attachment. The sign: you are more attached to the outcome than to their freedom.
→
Antidote: bring in karuṇā. Acknowledge their independent path and karma. Compassion restores warmth without grip.
"Metta is simply wishing well for others — a quality of caring about beings and their wellness. Rather than seeking to define it perfectly, we should seek to know and understand it experientially."

Karuṇā
Compassion
Mettā meeting suffering. The steady wish for beings to be free from pain — one hand reaching toward those in the current, the other anchored to the tree.
Near enemy
Grief or pity — collapsing into suffering
Far enemy
Cruelty or indifference to pain
Practice phrases

May I be free from pain and sorrow.
May I hold my own struggles with gentleness.
May I find relief and ease.

May you be free from pain and suffering.
May you be free from mental and physical distress.
May you find the relief you need.
May you be held in compassion.

May all beings who suffer in body find relief.
May all beings who suffer in mind find peace.
May compassion flow in all directions, without limit.

The river bank image
Karuṇā without upekkhā becomes grief — two people drowning rather than one rescued. The Buddha called wallowing "a bottomless pit." Steadiness is what makes genuine help possible.
→
Antidote: bring in muditā. Turn toward the person's strengths and goodness. You can hold both their suffering and their light.
"Compassion requires a strong, firm mindfulness — not a quaking mind — that keeps us steady and determined in the face of suffering."
Bhante Gunaratana · Bhavana Society

Muditā
Sympathetic joy
Mettā meeting happiness. Genuine delight in the joy and good fortune of others — their joy is complete in itself. I need not possess it to celebrate it.
Near enemy
Excitement or envy — coveting another's joy
Far enemy
Envy or resentment of others' happiness
Practice phrases

May my happiness grow and continue.
May joy arise easily in my heart.
May I find delight in small and large things alike.

May your happiness continue and grow.
I rejoice in your good fortune — may it increase.
May you not be separated from the happiness you have found.

May all beings who are happy continue in their happiness.
May joy abound in all directions, without limit.

The subtle test
Notice when admiration tips into comparison. Pure muditā is content with your own path. The moment you start wanting their circumstances — that is the near enemy speaking.
→
Antidote: bring in upekkhā. Recall impermanence. Their joy, like all things, will change. Find contentment with what is yours.
"Mudita practice helps us cultivate joy by setting the intention to be present for it. We begin to see more joy, and really find ourselves stopping to enjoy it."
Theravāda Tradition · Bhavana Society
Upekkhā
Equanimity
The crown of the four abodes — stable, warm, open-hearted presence. Not the absence of caring, but caring held with a steady, open hand. The mind like a clear sky.
Near enemy
Indifference — cold detachment dressed as wisdom
Far enemy
Restlessness, clinging, agitation
Practice phrases

All beings are the owners of their own karma.
Their happiness and suffering depend on their own actions.
I hold you with an open hand and an open heart.

May I not be swayed by gain and loss, praise and blame.
May the mind be spacious and the heart be free.

May all beings rest in balance — neither grasping nor pushing away.
May all beings everywhere abide in peace.

Upekkhā is the crown, not a bypass
Using "equanimity" to skip past mettā, karuṇā, or muditā is almost always indifference wearing spiritual clothing. True upekkhā carries all three within it, held lightly. Buddhaghosa: it can only fully arise after the other three are cultivated.
→
Antidote: return to mettā. Rekindle the basic wish — may this being be well and happy. Love without agenda is the ground of everything.
"Equanimity is not indifference — far from it. Without the other three it quickly becomes coldness. With them, it becomes the ground from which all genuine care flows."
Theravāda Tradition · Bhavana Society

Mettā → attachment

→ karuṇā
Karuṇā → grief
Bring muditā — turn toward their goodness
→ muditā
Muditā → intoxication
Bring upekkhā — recall impermanence
→ upekkhā
Upekkhā → indifference
Return to mettā — rekindle basic warmth
→ mettā
Mettā
Near: attachment
▾
The near enemy in practice
Possessive love — believing you know what is best for someone. Moving from wishing them well to needing them to follow a particular path. Conditional care with hidden strings.
"I care so much — they really should do it this way"
How to recognize it
Pure mettā has no agenda. The moment you feel hurt when someone chooses differently — the near enemy is present. You are more attached to the outcome than to their freedom.
Daily check
Am I wishing their happiness — or wishing my idea of their happiness?
→
Antidote: karuṇā. Acknowledge their independent suffering and path. Their karma is their own.
Karuṇā
Near: grief / pity
▾
Two faces of the near enemy
First: collapsing into suffering — being swept into the current. Second (subtler): a faint superiority — "poor soul." The grief in the second is hidden, emerging when the person finds your help intrusive.
"I feel so terrible for them I can't function"
The river bank
One arm reaches toward the person in the current — the other is anchored to the tree (upekkhā). Jumping in means two people drowning. The Buddha called wallowing "a bottomless pit."
Daily check
Am I holding their suffering with courage — or collapsing, or turning away?
→
Antidote: muditā. Turn toward their good qualities and resilience. Hold both their suffering and their goodness.
Muditā
Near: excitement
▾
The near enemy
Excitement or intoxication — swept up in another's joy in a way more about stimulation than genuine gladness. Also: coveting their circumstances, wanting to live their life rather than celebrating it.
"I'm happy for them — I wish I had what they have"
The subtle test
Pure muditā is content with your own path. Notice when admiration tips into comparison. Their joy is enough — complete in itself — you don't need it for yourself.
Daily check
Am I genuinely glad for their joy — or comparing it to what I have or lack?
→
Antidote: upekkhā. Recall impermanence. Find contentment with your own path.
Upekkhā
Near: indifference
▾
The most seductive near enemy
Indifference dressed as wisdom. Reciting "all beings have karma" without warmth — like a parrot, as Buddhaghosa wrote. The "nothing I can do" stance that slowly shuts the heart while appearing serene.
"I've accepted it — I feel nothing about it now"
The crown requires a foundation
Buddhaghosa: upekkhā reaches deep concentration only after the other three are cultivated. Using it to bypass mettā, karuṇā, or muditā is almost always the near enemy. True upekkhā carries all three within it.
Daily check
Am I at ease with what I can't control — or have I simply gone numb?
→
Antidote: mettā. Rekindle the basic wish: may this being be well and happy. Love without agenda is the ground.
